Part 1

Culture and Its Forms

Culture is the whole way of life of a group of people — their beliefs, customs, art, and social behaviour, passed down from one generation to the next. Sociologists often distinguish between material culture (the physical things a society produces, from buildings to fashion) and non-material culture (values, norms, and ideas).

Culture also takes many artistic forms. The visual arts include painting, sculpture, and photography. The performing arts cover theatre, dance, and music. Literature, cinema, architecture, and even cuisine are equally valid expressions of a culture's identity.

We also talk about high culture — traditionally associated with classical music, opera, or fine art — versus popular culture, which includes mainstream film, pop music, and social media trends. Neither is "better"; they simply appeal to different audiences and serve different social functions. Within any society, smaller subcultures also form around shared interests, from skateboarding to anime fandoms, each developing its own vocabulary, style, and values.

Part 2

Society, Culture and Art

Art rarely exists in a vacuum — it is shaped by, and helps shape, the society that produces it. Artists frequently respond to political events, social injustice, or technological change, turning their work into a form of commentary. Street art and protest music are obvious examples, but even a quiet landscape painting can reveal what a society values at a given moment.

Globalisation has made cultures more interconnected than ever. Streaming platforms expose teenagers to music and film from the other side of the world, and museums increasingly display art from distant cultures. This exchange enriches societies but also raises debates about cultural appropriation and the loss of local traditions.

Governments and institutions play a role too, through funding, censorship, or the preservation of cultural heritage. Whether a mural is protected as heritage or removed as vandalism often depends on whose voice is heard — a reminder that art and power are closely linked.

Part 3

Art and Personality Development

Beyond its social role, art shapes the individual. Engaging with art — whether by creating it or simply appreciating it — develops creativity, the ability to generate original ideas and see problems from unexpected angles. Analysing a poem or a painting also trains critical thinking, as students must justify an interpretation using evidence.

Art builds emotional intelligence. A novel can let a reader experience life from someone else's perspective, strengthening empathy. Music and drama, meanwhile, give people a safe outlet for emotions that might otherwise stay bottled up — this is the basic idea behind art therapy.

Finally, many of the soft skills employers value — collaboration, resilience after criticism, and the confidence to present an idea publicly — are quietly practised every time a student joins a choir, a drama club, or a life-drawing class. In short, art doesn't just decorate a personality; it helps build one.

Part 5 · Grammar

Language Lab: Reported Speech

Everything you need on indirect speech and reporting verbs — rules, exceptions, and things to watch out for.

What is reported speech?

Reported (or indirect) speech tells someone what another person said, without quoting their exact words. Compare:

Direct: "I love this exhibition," she said.
Reported: She said (that) she loved that exhibition.

Three things typically change: the verb tense (backshift), pronouns, and time/place expressions — because we are now speaking from a different moment and, often, a different place.

1. Tense backshift

Direct speechReported speech
Present simple→Past simple
Present continuous→Past continuous
Present perfect→Past perfect
Past simple→Past perfect
Past continuous→Past perfect continuous
will→would
can→could
may→might
must (obligation)→had to

2. Time, place and pronoun changes

DirectReported
now→then
today→that day
tomorrow→the next / following day
yesterday→the day before / the previous day
here→there
this / these→that / those
... ago→... before

Pronouns (I, my, you, your...) simply follow logic: they change to match who is speaking now and who is being talked about.

3. Say vs. tell

tell always needs an object (the listener): He told me (that) the museum was closing.
say does not take a direct object — if you name the listener, use "to": He said (to me) that the museum was closing.

Common error: "He said me that..." is wrong. Use told me or said to me.

4. Beyond "say" and "tell": reporting verb patterns

English has dozens of reporting verbs, and each follows one of a few grammar patterns. Learning the pattern is more useful than learning the verb alone.

PatternExample verbsExample sentence
verb + to-infinitiveagree, offer, promise, refuseShe agreed to join the art club.
verb + object + to-infinitiveadvise, warn, invite, orderHe advised us to visit the gallery early.
verb + -ingadmit, deny, suggest, recommendShe suggested visiting the new exhibition.
verb + (that) clauseclaim, explain, insist, complainHe complained that the tickets were expensive.

5. Reported questions

Yes/No questions — add if or whether, and switch back to statement word order (no auxiliary do/does/did):

"Do you like modern art?" → She asked if I liked modern art.

Wh-questions — keep the question word, but also use statement word order:

"Where did you buy that painting?" → He asked where I had bought that painting.

Common error: "She asked where did I buy it" — reported questions are not written as questions. No auxiliary do, and no question mark.

6. Reported commands, requests and suggestions

Use tell / ask / order + object + to-infinitive. For a negative, add not before to:

"Don't touch the paintings," the guard said. → The guard told us not to touch the paintings.

7. Exceptions — what to watch out for

Still true / general facts: if the statement is still true, backshift is optional. "The museum opens at nine," the guide said → The guide said the museum opens / opened at nine.
Reporting verb in the present: if say/tell itself is in the present tense (common in news, or when quoting something just said), the reported verb does not need to change. "Critics say the exhibition is a triumph."
Modals that don't change: could, would, should, might, ought to, used to, and mustn't stay the same in reported speech.
Time/place words only shift if the situation has changed — if you report a sentence on the same day, in the same place, "today" can stay "today."

8. State verbs — and why they matter here

English verbs are either state (stative) verbs — describing a condition, thought, feeling or sense rather than an action — or action (dynamic) verbs. State verbs are not normally used in continuous (-ing) forms.

CategoryExamples
Thoughts & opinionsbelieve, know, understand, think (= opinion), realise, doubt, suppose, agree
Feelings & wisheslove, like, hate, prefer, want, wish, need
Senses & perceptionsee, hear, smell, taste, feel (perceiving, not acting)
Possession & relationshave, own, belong, possess, contain, consist of
Being & measurementbe, seem, appear, weigh, cost, matter
Careful — some verbs can be either, depending on meaning:
think: "I think it's beautiful" (opinion, state) vs. "I'm thinking about the project" (process, action)
have: "I have two tickets" (possession, state) vs. "I'm having lunch" (activity, action)
see: "I see what you mean" (understand, state) vs. "I'm seeing an old friend tonight" (meeting, action)
taste / smell / look: "This soup tastes amazing" (state) vs. "The chef is tasting the soup" (action)
Reported speech connection: because state verbs don't take continuous forms, they backshift straight from present simple to past simple — never to past continuous. "I love this painting," she said → She said she loved that painting — not "was loving."

9. Intensifiers

Intensifiers are words that make an adjective, verb, or adverb stronger. Which one you choose depends on whether the adjective is gradable (has degrees) or non-gradable / extreme (already at its maximum).

Adjective typeTypical intensifiersExample
Gradablevery, really, quite, rather, fairly, extremelyvery beautiful, quite interesting
Non-gradable / extremeabsolutely, completely, totally, utterly, reallyabsolutely stunning, utterly captivating

Common gradable → extreme pairs: good → excellent · bad → terrible · big → huge/enormous · interesting → fascinating · tired → exhausted · surprised → astonished · beautiful → stunning/breathtaking

Common error: "very stunning" sounds wrong — very doesn't usually combine with extreme adjectives. Use absolutely stunning instead. Really is the flexible exception — it works with both types.
Reported speech connection: intensifiers don't change when you report someone's words — only the verb tense, pronouns and time expressions shift. "This exhibition is absolutely incredible!" she said → She said (that) the exhibition was absolutely incredible.

Applying for a Job at a Cultural Institution

Role-play

Read your role card and act out a short job interview for a Gallery Assistant / Tour Guide position (6–8 exchanges). Then write a reported-speech summary of the interview using at least six different reporting verbs.

Role A — Interviewer

  • Ask why the candidate wants to work at the museum.
  • Ask about experience with visitors or public speaking.
  • Ask how they would handle a difficult or upset visitor.
  • Ask if they are available to work weekends.
  • Offer the candidate a trial shift.

Role B — Candidate

  • Explain your interest in art / culture / languages.
  • Admit you have limited experience, but mention a related skill.
  • Promise to be reliable and to learn quickly.
  • Politely ask about the salary or working hours.
  • Decide whether to accept the trial shift.

What Should We Wear?

Role-play / peer discussion

In small groups, discuss Scenario A or Scenario B using the sentence starters from the Language Lab (opinion, agreeing, disagreeing). Then find a new partner and report back what your group members thought — use at least four different reporting verbs (e.g. thought, suggested, insisted, pointed out).

Scenario A — Ballet at the National Theatre

  • Is a suit / dress expected, or is smart-casual fine?
  • Are jeans and trainers acceptable?
  • Does the season or type of performance change the dress code?
  • What would you avoid wearing, and why?

Scenario B — Folk Festival

  • Is traditional dress appropriate, or only for performers?
  • What should you consider for a whole day outdoors?
  • Comfortable shoes vs. "festival style" — which matters more?
  • Would you dress differently for a folk festival than for the ballet? Why?

Reporting-back starter: "In my group, ... pointed out that ... . Someone else suggested that ..., but ... insisted that ... ."

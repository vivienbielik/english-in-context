Culture and Art

A reading text on culture, art and its forms — with comprehension exercises, reported-verb practice, and a paragraph-reference challenge.

Reading text
1

Culture is the whole way of life of a group of people — their beliefs, values, customs and shared behaviours, passed down from one generation to the next. Art, meanwhile, is usually defined as the visual, literary and performing work created by individuals or groups for aesthetic or intellectual pleasure. The two concepts overlap constantly: art is often described as a product of culture, while culture is the context in which art is created and experienced. It is not always clear which one shapes the other — a society's art can just as easily influence its culture as the reverse.

2

Art takes three broad forms. Visual art includes architecture, ceramics, drawing, filmmaking, painting, photography and sculpting. Performing art covers dance, music, theatre, opera and mime. Literary art includes prose, poetry and drama. Each of these categories has evolved its own conventions, but they frequently borrow from one another — a piece of theatre, for instance, brings together performance, literary script and often visual design all at once.

3

Art rarely exists in a vacuum. Artists frequently respond to political events, social injustice or technological change, turning their work into a form of commentary — street art and protest music are obvious examples. Globalisation has also made cultures more interconnected than ever: streaming platforms expose audiences to music and film from around the world, and museums increasingly display work from distant cultures. This exchange enriches societies, though it also raises debates about cultural appropriation and the loss of local traditions. Governments and institutions play a role too, through funding, censorship or heritage protection — whether a mural is preserved as heritage or removed as vandalism often depends on whose voice is heard.

4

Beyond its social role, art shapes the individual. Engaging with art — whether by creating it or simply appreciating it — develops creativity and trains critical thinking, since interpreting a poem or a painting requires justifying a reading with evidence. Art also builds emotional intelligence: a novel can let a reader experience life from someone else's perspective, and music or drama gives people a safe outlet for emotions that might otherwise stay bottled up. Many of the soft skills employers value — collaboration, resilience after criticism, the confidence to present an idea publicly — are quietly practised every time a student joins a choir, a drama club or a life-drawing class.

5

Three well-known artists have each described art rather differently. Leonardo da Vinci reportedly said that "art is never finished, only abandoned," suggesting that a work of art can always, in theory, be developed further. Georges Braque argued that the only thing of real value in art is what cannot be explained, pointing to the idea that art works on a level language cannot fully capture. Henri Matisse, for his part, insisted that creativity takes courage — a reminder that making art always involves a degree of risk.

6

Whether or not someone considers themselves "artistic," art surrounds daily life — in the design of a building, the soundtrack of a film, or the cover of a book. Many educators argue that studying art at school matters precisely because it does not have a single correct answer: it teaches young people to tolerate ambiguity, defend an interpretation and see the world from more than one angle at once.

Exercise A · Reported verbs

Report the quote correctly

Read each direct quote, then choose the sentence that reports it correctly — pay attention to the reporting verb's pattern (that / -ing / to + infinitive).

1. A curator said to a group of students:

"I recommend visiting the exhibition on your own first, before the guided tour."

Correct: recommend + -ing. "Recommended to visit" and "recommended visit" are not valid patterns; "recommended that visiting" mixes two structures.

2. An art critic told a reporter:

"I promise I'll write an honest review, even if it upsets the artist."

Correct: promise + to + infinitive — a future commitment.

3. A student admitted to her teacher:

"Honestly, I don't fully understand modern art."

Correct: admit can take "that" + clause (fact) or "to" + -ing — here the "that" version fits best.

4. A gallery guide insisted to visitors:

"Please, don't touch the sculptures — I really must ask you to keep your distance."

Correct: insist ON + -ing — the hidden preposition rule.

5. A teacher suggested to the class:

"Why don't we compare a printed book with an audiobook version of the same story?"

Correct: suggest + -ing (suggest never takes "to + infinitive" or an object before the verb).

Part 1 · Overview

All Conditionals at a Glance

A conditional sentence has two parts: the if-clause (the condition) and the main clause (the result). The tenses you choose show how real the situation is and when it happens.

TypeIf-clauseMain clauseMeaningExample
Zeropresent simplepresent simplealways true: facts, rules, habitsIf you heat ice, it melts.
Firstpresent simplewill / can / may / might + infinitivereal, possible futureIf it rains, we will stay in.
Secondpast simple (were)would / could / might + infinitiveunreal present or unlikely futureIf I had more time, I would learn Spanish.
Thirdpast perfectwould / could / might + have + past participleunreal past: things that did not happenIf we had left earlier, we wouldn't have missed the train.
Mixed 3 + 2past perfectwould / could / might + infinitiveunreal past → present resultIf I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now.
Mixed 2 + 3past simple (were)would / could / might + have + past participleunreal present (permanent fact) → past resultIf I were braver, I would have spoken to her yesterday.

Punctuation: use a comma when the if-clause comes first: If it rains, we'll stay in. No comma when the main clause comes first: We'll stay in if it rains.

Part 2 · Rules & Examples

The Six Types

For each type: the form, when to use it, examples, and the detail that examiners look for.

0Zero conditional

always / in general
If / When + present simple, present simple

Facts, scientific truths, rules and habits. If means the same as when / whenever.

  • If you mix red and white, you get pink.
  • Water boils if you heat it to 100 °C.
  • If my brother gets home late, he doesn't eat dinner.

Tip: both clauses use the same present tense. Never use will for facts: it will melt → it melts.

1First conditional

real future
If + present simple, will / can / may / might / should + infinitive (or an imperative)

A real possibility in the future: something that may well happen. Also used for promises, warnings and threats.

  • If you revise regularly, you will pass the Maturita.
  • If the weather improves, we might go hiking.
  • If you see Peter, tell him to call me.
  • If you have finished, you can leave. (present perfect in the if-clause)

Tip: will almost never goes in the if-clause: If it will rain → If it rains. The same rule applies after when, as soon as, unless, before, until, provided.

2Second conditional

unreal nowunlikely future
If + past simple, would / could / might + infinitive

Imaginary situations in the present, or future situations we think are unlikely. The past tense does not mean past time here; it means "not real".

  • If I had a car, I would drive to school. (I don't have one.)
  • If I were you, I would apply for the scholarship. (advice)
  • What would you do if you won the lottery?
  • If he could speak French, he might get the job in Brussels.

Tip: in formal English use were for all persons: If she were here… Was is common in speech, but If I were you is fixed.

3Third conditional

unreal past
If + past perfect, would / could / might + have + past participle

Imagining a different past: situations that did not happen. Used for regrets, criticism and relief.

  • If I had known about the test, I would have studied. (I didn't know.)
  • She wouldn't have got lost if she had taken a map.
  • If you hadn't helped me, I couldn't have finished on time.

Tip: 'd can mean had (if-clause) or would (main clause): If I'd known, I'd have come. = If I had known, I would have come. Never write If I would have known.

3+2Mixed: past condition → present result

past → now
If + past perfect, would / could / might + infinitive

An unreal past event and its consequence now. Look for time words such as now, today, still, at the moment.

  • If I had accepted the job in London, I would be living abroad now.
  • If she hadn't broken her leg, she would be in the team today.
  • We wouldn't be lost if you had listened to me.

Check it: past fact (I didn't accept the job) + present fact (I am not living abroad).

2+3Mixed: present condition → past result

always / now → past
If + past simple, would / could / might + have + past participle

A permanent unreal situation (character, ability, general fact) that explains a past result.

  • If I were more organised, I wouldn't have lost my passport last week.
  • If he spoke German, he would have got the job in Vienna.
  • If she weren't so shy, she would have sung at the concert.

Check it: present/general fact (I am not organised) + past fact (I lost my passport).

Part 3 · Mixed Conditionals in Focus

How to Build a Mixed Conditional

Write down the two real facts first, decide when each one is true, and then choose the tense for each clause separately.

Mixed 3 + 2 · step by step Facts: I didn't save money last year (past). I can't buy a laptop now (present).
Past fact → past perfect: If I had saved money last year,
Present result → would + infinitive: I could buy a laptop now.
Mixed 2 + 3 · step by step Facts: I'm not a confident speaker (always). I didn't volunteer to present yesterday (past).
Present fact → past simple: If I were a more confident speaker,
Past result → would have + past participle: I would have volunteered to present yesterday.

Part 4 · Advanced Forms (C1)

Inversion and Alternatives to if

These structures show the examiner a wide range of grammar, so use one or two in your composition.

Inverted conditionals (formal)

Drop if and put the auxiliary before the subject.

TypeWith ifInverted
FirstIf you should need any help, call me.Should you need any help, call me.
SecondIf I were in your position, I would refuse.Were I in your position, I would refuse.
Second (were to)If the company were to close, 200 people would lose their jobs.Were the company to close, 200 people would lose their jobs.
ThirdIf we had known, we would have come.Had we known, we would have come.
NegativeIf it hadn't been for you, I would have failed.Had it not been for you, I would have failed. (not ✗ Hadn't it been)

Alternatives to if

ExpressionMeaningExample
unlessif … not / except ifUnless you hurry, you'll miss the bus. (not ✗ Unless you don't hurry)
provided / providing (that), as long as, on condition thatonly if (a strong condition)You can borrow my bike as long as you bring it back tonight.
in caseas a precaution; not the same as ifTake an umbrella in case it rains. (take it now, rain or no rain)
suppose / supposing / imaginelet's imagineSupposing you lost your phone, what would you do?
otherwise / or (else)if not, then…Write it down, otherwise you'll forget it.
but for / without + nounif it weren't / hadn't been forBut for your help, I would never have finished the project.
if it weren't for / if it hadn't been forif something didn't exist / hadn't happenedIf it weren't for music, my life would be boring.
even ifthe result doesn't changeI won't tell you, even if you beg me.

Will / would in an if-clause? Only in special meanings: politeness (If you will wait here, the doctor will see you soon; If you would sign here, please), insistence (If you will eat so much junk food, of course you'll feel ill) or a result in the future (I'll give you 10 euros if it will help).

For I wish / If only (regrets and wishes), see the Young People and Society: Wish Clauses & Conditionals module.

Part 5 · Common Mistakes

Mistakes That Cost Points

  • If it will rain tomorrow, … → If it rains tomorrow, … No will in the if-clause.
  • If I would have known, … → If I had known, … No would in the if-clause.
  • If I would be rich, … → If I were rich, …
  • Unless you don't study, you'll fail. → Unless you study, you'll fail. Unless is already negative.
  • If he had took the job… → If he had taken the job… Use the past participle after had.
  • I would of helped you. → I would have helped you. would've sounds like "would of", but it isn't.
  • I'll take a jacket if it gets cold. (when you mean a precaution) → I'll take a jacket in case it gets cold.
  • If she studies harder, she would pass. → If she studies harder, she will pass. / If she studied harder, she would pass. Don't mix the first and second conditionals.

Maturita tip: in the written composition (PFIČ), one well-chosen second, third or mixed conditional (or an inverted one, such as Had I known…) shows a wider range of structures under criterion III (Grammar). In the Language in Use test, watch out for unless, in case, provided and otherwise.

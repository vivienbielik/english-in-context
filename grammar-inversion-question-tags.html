Part 1 · Basics

What Is Inversion?

Inversion means changing the normal English word order (subject + verb) so that the verb, or part of it, comes before the subject.

Normal orderI have never seen such chaos.
InversionNever have I seen such chaos.

Two kinds of inversion

1. Auxiliary inversion (most common): auxiliary / modal / be + subject + main verb.
Rarely does he complain. · Never will I forget it. · Not only was she late…

If the sentence has no auxiliary, add do / does / did, just like in questions:
He rarely complains → Rarely does he complain. (not ✗ Rarely complains he)

2. Full inversion: the whole verb comes before a noun subject, with no do.
Here comes the bus. · On the hill stood a castle. · “Help!” shouted the boy.

Questions: the everyday inversion

TypeRuleExample
Yes/No questionsaux + S + VHave you finished? Does she speak French?
Wh- questionsWh- + aux + S + VWhere did you go? Why is he crying?
Subject questions ⚠️NO inversion, no doWho called you? What happened? (not ✗ Who did call you?)
Indirect questions ⚠️NO inversionCan you tell me where the station is? (not ✗ where is the station)
Part 2 · Grammar Lab

14 Types of Inversion

Each card shows the structure, examples and the most common trap. Types 1–10 are the ones most often tested at C1 level.

1Negative & limiting adverbials

Never / Rarely / Seldom / Hardly ever / Nowhere + auxiliary + subject
  • Never have I seen such a beautiful sunset.
  • Rarely do teenagers read printed newspapers today.
  • Nowhere else can you find such fresh seafood.

⚠️ Use do / does / did when there is no auxiliary: Seldom does she complain.

2Negative phrases with “no”

At no time / Under no circumstances / On no account / In no way / By no means + aux + S
  • Under no circumstances should you share your password.
  • At no time was the patient in danger.
  • On no account must this door be left open.

⚠️ Typical in rules, warnings and formal letters.

3Not only … but (also)

Not only + aux + S + V, but S (also) + V
  • Not only did she win the race, but she also broke the record.
  • Not only is the app free, but it is also safe.

⚠️ Only the first part is inverted. The part after but has normal word order.

4Hardly / Scarcely / Barely … when · No sooner … than

Hardly + had + S + PP … when + past simple
  • Hardly had we arrived when it started to rain.
  • No sooner had the concert started than the power went off.
  • Scarcely had I sat down when the phone rang.

⚠️ Hardly … when, but No sooner … than. Don't mix them! Usually with the Past Perfect.

5Only + time / manner expressions

Only after / Only when / Only then / Only by / Only in this way / Only if + … + aux + S
  • Only after the exam did I realise my mistake.
  • Only by working together can we solve the problem.
  • Only then did he understand the truth.

⚠️ With Only after / when / if / by + clause, the inversion is in the main clause: Only when I left did I notice…

6Not until / Not till

Not until + time / clause + aux + S + V
  • Not until 1969 did a human walk on the Moon.
  • Not until I read the book did I understand the film.

⚠️ Inversion is in the second (main) clause, never after until.

7Little

Little + aux + S + V (= S didn't realise / had no idea)
  • Little did I know that my life was about to change.
  • Little do they realise how much the project costs.

⚠️ Used with verbs of knowing: know, realise, suspect, imagine, understand.

8Conditional inversion (no “if”)

Had + S + PP · Were + S + to / adj · Should + S + V
  • Had I known about the test, I would have studied. (= If I had known)
  • Were I you, I would apply. / Were the price to rise, …
  • Should you need help, please call us. (= If you need)
  • Had it not been for the doctor, he would have died.

⚠️ Negatives are not contracted: Had it not been…, never ✗ Hadn't it been…

9So / Such … that

So + adj / adv + aux + S … that · Such + be + noun … that
  • So difficult was the test that nobody finished it.
  • So quickly did the news spread that the website crashed.
  • Such was the demand that tickets sold out in minutes.

⚠️ Very emphatic, typical of literature and formal speeches.

10So / Neither / Nor (short responses)

So / Neither / Nor + aux + S
  • “I love jazz.” – “So do I.”
  • “I can't swim.” – “Neither can my brother.”
  • He didn't call, nor did he send a message.

⚠️ Positive → So. Negative → Neither / Nor. The auxiliary must match: “I've finished.” – “So have I.”

11Full inversion after place & direction adverbials

Here / There / Down / Up / Out / Adverbial of place + verb + noun subject
  • Here comes the bus!
  • Down came the rain.
  • On the hill stood an old castle.
  • In the corner of the room sat a tiny black cat.

⚠️ The whole verb goes before the subject (no do). With a pronoun subject, there is no inversion: Here it comes, not ✗ Here comes it.

12Comparisons with “as” and “than”

as / than + aux + noun subject
  • She works in Brussels, as does her husband.
  • Teenagers use social media more than do their parents.

⚠️ Optional and formal. With pronouns, keep normal order: …than they do.

13Reporting verbs after direct speech

“…,” + verb + noun subject
  • “Stop talking!” shouted the teacher.
  • “We did it!” cried the team.

⚠️ Common in stories. With pronouns, use normal order: “Stop!” she shouted.

14Wishes and exclamations

May + S + V! · Long live …! · Negative question as exclamation
  • May all your dreams come true!
  • Long live the King!
  • Isn't it a wonderful day!

⚠️ Formal, fixed expressions, typical for toasts, cards and speeches.

Part 3 · Usage

How and When to Use Inversion

Inversion is mainly used for emphasis and in a formal style: essays, speeches, formal letters, news, literature and exam writing tasks.

SituationUseful inversionExample
Formal essay (maturita writing)Not only…, Rarely…, Only by…Only by investing in education can we reduce poverty.
Formal letter / emailShould you…, Were you to…Should you have any questions, do not hesitate to contact me.
Rules and warningsUnder no circumstances…, On no account…On no account should students use phones during the exam.
StorytellingHardly… when, No sooner… than, Little did…Little did she know that her life would change forever.
Everyday conversationSo do I, Neither can I, Here comes…“I'm tired.” – “So am I.”

Exceptions: when NOT to invert

“Only” + subject: if only describes the subject, there is no inversion. Only Peter knew the answer. (not ✗ Only did Peter know)
Negative word not at the start: I have never seen it. Inversion happens only when the negative expression starts the clause.
“Not until” / “Only after” + clause: the inversion is in the main clause, not in the time clause. Not until he left did I relax. (not ✗ Not until did he leave…)
Pronoun subjects with full inversion: Here it comes. Off they went. “Wait!” he said.
Time phrases with “no”: In no time means “very quickly”, not a negative, so there is no inversion: In no time we had finished. Compare: At no time did we give up.
Contractions in conditional inversion: Had it not been for… ✓   ✗ Hadn't it been for…
💡 Exam hack: In keyword transformations, if the key word is NEVER, RARELY, SOONER, ONLY, UNTIL, LITTLE, HAD, SHOULD or SUCH, think “inversion” first. Then check: did I add do/does/did? Is the tense the same as in the original?
Part 4 · Question Tags

Question Tags: Rules & Exceptions

A question tag is a short question at the end of a sentence. It is also a form of inversion: auxiliary + pronoun.

Basic rules

1. Positive sentence → negative tag: You're coming, aren't you?
2. Negative sentence → positive tag: She can't drive, can she?
3. Use the same auxiliary or modal. If there is none, use do / does / did: He plays the guitar, doesn't he?
4. Use a pronoun, not the noun: Your sister lives in Nitra, doesn't she?

Intonation: ↘ falling = you expect agreement (It's cold, isn't it? ↘). ↗ rising = a real question, you are not sure (You've paid, haven't you? ↗).

Same-way tags (positive + positive): used to show surprise, interest or sarcasm, not to ask. So you've bought a new phone, have you? / Oh, you're a genius, are you?
Contracted tags: negative tags are almost always contracted: isn't it, don't you. Full form (does he not?) is very formal.

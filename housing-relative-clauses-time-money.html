💡 Watch out: easily confused words

  • a flat (BrE) / an apartment (AmE) = byt · a block of flats = bytovka · a housing estate = sídlisko.
  • a landlord owns and rents out · a tenant pays rent · a lodger rents a room in the owner's own home.
  • rent (nájomné, prenajať si) · rent out / let (prenajať niekomu): We rent a flat. Our landlord lets it to students.
  • a deposit = kaucia (renting) or akontácia (buying) · a down payment = akontácia only.
  • economic (hospodársky) · economical (úsporný: an economical heating system).
  • move house (no article!) · move in · move out · move to + place.
  • house = the building · home = where you live and feel you belong: A house is not always a home.

📰 Reading: Home, Sweet Smart Home – Living in 2050

Read the article about the homes of the future. Notice the relative clauses – you will analyse them in Exercise 7.

1Imagine waking up in a house that knows exactly when you need your coffee. By 2050, homes which seem futuristic today will be completely ordinary. Architects, many of whom already design zero-energy buildings, believe that the biggest change will not be clever gadgets, but the way our homes produce and save energy.

2The passive house, which was first tested in Germany around 1990, is a good example. Thanks to thick insulation, triple glazing and an airtight shell, it needs up to 90% less heating than a conventional building. Fresh air is supplied by a ventilation system that recovers heat from the stale air leaving the house. Owners who live in such homes often say that their heating bills are lower than their phone bills.

3Cities are also looking upwards. Green roofs, where grass, herbs and even vegetables grow, cool buildings in summer and absorb rainwater during heavy storms. In Milan, two residential towers covered with hundreds of trees, known as the Bosco Verticale, have become a symbol of greener city living.

4Building methods are changing too. Some companies are already printing small houses layer by layer from concrete, a process which can cut construction time from months to days. Others build modular homes in factories, from which complete rooms are transported to the site and assembled like Lego. Supporters say this is the only realistic way to provide the affordable housing that young people desperately need.

5Not everyone is convinced, though. Critics, whose main worry is cost, point out that heat pumps and solar panels still require a large investment, which many families simply cannot afford. And as urban planners like to remind us, the real challenge is not building new eco-homes but retrofitting the millions of draughty old houses where most of us actually live. Saving energy, after all, starts with the homes we already have.

🔗 Relative Clauses: Defining and Non-defining

Relative clauses give more information about a noun. The key question is always: is the information essential, or is it extra?

PronounUsed forExample
whopeople (subject or object)The agent who sold us the flat…
whompeople – object, formal; always after prepositionsThe tenant to whom I spoke…
whichthings, animals, ideas; a whole clauseThe flat which we rent… / …, which was a shock.
thatpeople or things – defining clauses onlyThe heat pump that we bought…
whosepossession (people and things)The family whose house burned down…
whereplaces (= in / at which)The town where I grew up…
whentimes (= on / in which)The year when we moved…
whyafter the reasonThe reason why rents are rising…

Defining relative clause

Identifies which person or thing we mean. Without it, the sentence is unclear.

  • No commas
  • who, which or that
  • Object pronoun can be left out

The flat (that) we saw yesterday is already rented.

People who live in passive houses pay very little for heating.

Non-defining relative clause

Adds extra information about something already identified. The sentence works without it.

  • Commas (pauses in speech)
  • who, which, whose… – never that
  • Pronoun can never be left out

Our flat, which we bought in 2019, has a balcony.

Bratislava, where rents are highest, attracts young people.

⚖️ Commas change the meaning

My sister who lives in Bratislava rents a studio flat. → I have more than one sister; I mean the one in Bratislava.
My sister, who lives in Bratislava, rents a studio flat. → I have only one sister; where she lives is extra information.
The tenants who had paid their rent were allowed to stay. → only some tenants.
The tenants, who had paid their rent, were allowed to stay. → all of them had paid.

✂️ When can you leave out the pronoun?

defining clause + pronoun = OBJECT → can be left out
The house (that) we bought needs a new roof. (we is the subject, that = object) ✅
The man who sold us the house has moved abroad. (who = subject) ❌ cannot be left out

📌 Prepositions in relative clauses

informal: the flat (that) I live in · formal: the flat in which I live
the landlord (who) we complained to = the landlord to whom we complained (formal)
Never: the flat in that I live ❌ · the landlord to who ❌ · the town where I live in ❌

🧮 Quantifiers + of whom / of which / of whose

some / many / most / all / none / both / neither / one + of whom (people) / of which (things)
We viewed ten flats, none of which had a balcony.
The residents, most of whom are pensioners, voted against the plan.
Our neighbours, one of whose children goes to my school, are moving out.

💬 Sentential which – commenting on the whole idea

clause + , which + comment
The rent went up by 20%, which means we'll have to move.
They installed solar panels, which cut their bills by half.
❌ …, what means… ❌ …, that means…

🚀 C1 level: reduced relative clauses

who/which + active verb → -ING · who/which + be + past participle → 3rd form · the first/last/only + to-infinitive
People living in tiny houses… (= who live)
Houses built before 1950… (= which were built)
She was the first person to install a heat pump in our street. (= who installed)

⚠️ Typical mistakes of Slovak students

  • The house what I bought… → The house which/that I bought… (what = the thing that – never after a noun)
  • My mother, that is a teacher, … → My mother, who is a teacher, …
  • The flat which I rented it… → The flat which I rented… (don't repeat the object – which already replaces it)
  • …, most of them were too expensive. → …, most of which were too expensive.
  • The man who's car was stolen… → The man whose car was stolen…

⏳ Verbs of Time and Money

Renting, buying and running a home is all about time and money. These verbs look simple, but their patterns are a classic Maturita trap.

spend · take

  • person + spend + time + -ing: I spent a week painting the flat.
  • it takes (+ person) + time + to do: It took me a week to paint the flat.
  • spend money on sth: We spent €900 on furniture.

cost · afford · pay

  • sth costs (sb) + money: It cost us €500.
  • can('t) afford sth / to do sth: We can't afford to move.
  • pay for sth: Who paid for the repairs? but pay the rent / a bill (no for)

borrow · lend · owe

  • borrow sth from sb (you get): I borrowed €200 from Dad.
  • lend sb sth / sth to sb (you give): Dad lent me €200.
  • owe sb money: I owe Dad €200.

pay off · pay back · save

  • pay off a debt / mortgage = pay it all
  • pay sb back = return borrowed money
  • save (up) for sth · set / put aside money · save time / energy

Spending (too much)

  • splash out on = spend a lot on a treat
  • fork out = pay unwillingly
  • fritter away = waste slowly
  • run up debts / a bill

Spending less & managing

  • cut back on = reduce
  • run out of = have none left
  • chip in = contribute a little
  • make ends meet · budget for

Time

  • waste time on / -ing · kill time
  • make the most of your time
  • free up time · run out of time
  • be short of / pressed for time

Discussion Lab

Three big housing decisions

Work in pairs or small groups. Choose one decision, add your own arguments and reach a conclusion together.

🔑 Rent or buy?

+ Renting: flexibility, no debt, repairs are the landlord's job

− Renting: money you never get back, landlord can raise the rent

+ Buying: your own property, freedom to renovate

− Buying: a mortgage for 30 years, a big down payment

🏙️ City flat or house in the countryside?

+ City: everything within walking distance, jobs, culture

− City: soaring rents, noise, cramped flats

+ Countryside: space, a garden, fresh air

− Countryside: commuting time, fewer services

👨‍👩‍👧 Stay with parents or move out?

+ Staying: you can save up for a deposit

− Staying: less independence and privacy

+ Moving out: freedom, learning to budget

− Moving out: rent and utility bills, harder to make ends meet

🗨️ Useful phrases

  • Giving opinions: As far as I'm concerned, … · The way I see it, … · I'm convinced that …
  • Agreeing / disagreeing: That's a fair point, but … · I see what you mean, however … · I couldn't agree more.
  • Reaching a decision: On balance, … · All things considered, we think … · So, shall we agree that …?

Answer in 1–2 minutes each

Your home

  • Describe the home you live in. What do you like most about it?
  • What would you change if money were no problem?
  • Describe a room that is special to you.

Young people & housing

  • Why is it so difficult for young people to get on the property ladder?
  • At what age should young people move out? Why?
  • Would you share a flat with friends? What could go wrong?

The future

  • What will homes look like in 2050?
  • Which eco-friendly solutions would you install in your home and why?
  • Should the government pay for insulation and solar panels?

Society

  • What can towns do about homelessness?
  • Is gentrification good or bad for a neighbourhood?
  • Would you live in a tiny house or a co-housing community?

Reporting verbs: that, -ing, or to + infinitive?

When we report what someone said, we usually choose a reporting verb (say, admit, promise, suggest...). But different reporting verbs are followed by different structures. Knowing which structure goes with which verb is the key to sounding natural in reported speech.

1. Verb + that

We use that when the reporting verb simply introduces a fact or a statement — a full sentence someone said or believes.

She admitted (that) she had made a mistake.
He explained that the museum was closed on Mondays.

Note: "that" can often be left out in informal English — both versions are correct.

2. Verb + -ing

Many of these verbs contain a hidden preposition just before the -ing form (apologise for, insist on, accuse someone of). Once you spot the preposition, the rule is automatic: a preposition is always followed by -ing, never by the infinitive.

He apologised for being late.
She insisted on paying for dinner.
They accused him of lying.

3. Verb + to + infinitive

We use this structure for promises, plans, offers, and orders — actions that are going to happen, not actions that already happened.

He promised to call her back.
She agreed to help with the project.
The teacher ordered him to leave the room.

Verbs that belong to more than one group

Some verbs can follow more than one pattern, depending on meaning or on what exactly is being reported: admit, agree, claim, deny, insist, mention, promise, recommend, suggest, warn. For example:

She suggested that we leave early. (a general recommendation)
She suggested leaving early. (the same idea, -ing form)

The memory hack

Fact → that. Hidden preposition → -ing. Future action → to.

Quick check

Before you use a reporting verb, ask yourself: Am I reporting a fact someone stated (that)? Is there a preposition hiding before the verb (-ing)? Or is this about something that will happen next (to)?

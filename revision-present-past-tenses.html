Grammar 1

Revision of Present and Past Tenses

Six tenses carry most of what you say and write at B2 level – two present, two past, and the perfect forms that connect them. The choice between them is rarely about grammar rules alone; it’s about the speaker’s point of view – is the action finished, ongoing, or does it link to another moment in time?

1. Present Simple vs. Present Continuous

Present Simple describes facts, routines, permanent situations, and timetables – things that are true in general, not just right now.

She teaches at the secondary grammar school. · The museum opens at nine.

Present Continuous describes actions happening at the moment of speaking, temporary situations, and changing or developing trends.

I ’m writing a new worksheet right now. · More schools are introducing CLIL this year.

always · usually · every day · on Mondays · seldom · rarely · scarcely now · at the moment · currently · these days

Note: verbs describing states rather than actions (know, believe, want, seem, belong) are rarely used in the continuous form.

  • He rarely eats breakfast before school.
  • The 7:45 train departs from platform 4 every morning.
  • My brother is studying medicine in Bratislava this year.
  • Ice melts at zero degrees Celsius.
  • Online sales are rising sharply this quarter.
  • I usually walk to work, but today I’m driving because it’s raining.
  • She seldom arrives late for class.
  • They are currently renovating the school library.
  • He scarcely ever complains about his workload.
  • We’re meeting the new Erasmus+ coordinator tomorrow at nine.
  • She scarcely revises for the final exams, though her success so far has gone unnoticed.

Seldom in affirmative, negative, and question forms – seldom is a near-negative adverb (like rarely and scarcely), so it isn’t normally combined with not. For a true negative, use doesn’t/don’t … often instead.

  • Affirmative: She seldom eats fast food.
  • Negative (equivalent meaning): She doesn’t often eat fast food.
  • Question: Does she seldom eat fast food? / How often does she eat fast food?
  • Affirmative: My father seldom watches television during the week.
  • Negative (equivalent meaning): My father doesn’t usually watch television during the week.
  • Question: How often does your father watch television during the week?
  • Affirmative: We seldom argue about small things.
  • Negative (equivalent meaning): We don’t often argue about small things.
  • Question: Do you seldom argue about small things?

Quick practice – Present Simple vs. Present Continuous

1. My colleagues usually (mark) exams by hand, but this term they (use) an online system.
2. Look! The Erasmus+ coordinator (upload) the new photos to the website right now.
3. Our school (organise) an international project every two years.

2. Past Simple vs. Past Continuous

Past Simple describes completed actions or a sequence of events in the past.

She visited Vienna last spring. · He finished the report before lunch.

Past Continuous describes an action in progress at a specific past moment, often as background to another event.

I was preparing the lesson when the power went out. · While she was marking tests, the phone rang.

yesterday · last year · in 2015 · ago while · when · at 8 o’clock yesterday

Note: the two forms often work together – the continuous sets the scene, the simple interrupts it.

  • She was cooking dinner when the phone rang.
  • We visited three countries during our Erasmus+ exchange.
  • While I was marking tests, my colleague was preparing the timetable.
  • He broke his leg while he was skiing in the Tatras.
  • They arrived at the airport just as the gates were closing.
  • I was reading a book when the lights went out.
  • The students were rehearsing the play all afternoon.
  • She graduated from university in 2011.
  • It was raining heavily when we left the building.
  • He finished the report before his manager asked for it.

Quick practice – Past Simple vs. Past Continuous

1. While the students (rehearse) for the concert, the fire alarm (go) off.
2. I (walk) home when I (see) my old teacher.
3. At eight o’clock last night, my grandmother (watch) the news, as she always did.

3. Present Perfect Simple vs. Past Simple

Present Perfect connects the past to now – a result that matters today, an unfinished period, or an unspecified time.

I ’ve already sent the report. · She has taught here for over thirty years.

Past Simple is used instead whenever the sentence names a specific, finished point in the past.

I sent the report yesterday. · She started teaching in 1994.

already · just · yet · ever · never · since · for · so far yesterday · in 2020 · when I was at school

Note: a stated past time word (yesterday, in 2020…) always rules out the Present Perfect.

  • I have never been to Japan.
  • She visited Japan three years ago.
  • We have just finished the Erasmus+ report.
  • They finished the report yesterday afternoon.
  • Have you ever tried Slovak halušky?
  • I tried halušky for the first time last summer.
  • He has lived in Levice since 2005.
  • He lived in Bratislava for five years before moving here.
  • Our school has organised twelve Erasmus+ projects so far.
  • Our school organised its first Erasmus+ project in 2013.

Quick practice – Present Perfect Simple vs. Past Simple

1. We (already / send) the permission forms to the parents.
2. Erasmus+ (change) the way our school works since we joined in 2013.
3. She (graduate) from university in 2010.

4. Present Perfect Continuous

This form highlights the duration of an activity that began in the past and continues now – or has just stopped, leaving a visible result.

I ’ve been correcting essays since seven o’clock. · Her eyes are red – she ’s been crying.

for · since · all day · recently · lately

Note: compare with Present Perfect Simple, which focuses on the completed result rather than the duration – I’ve been reading that book (still going) vs. I’ve read that book (finished).

  • I’ve been waiting for this email all week.
  • She’s been feeling tired lately.
  • We’ve been planning this trip since January.
  • He’s been coughing for three days now.
  • They’ve been renovating the gym for months.
  • How long have you been learning Slovak?
  • I’ve been trying to reach you all morning.
  • The children have been playing outside since lunchtime.
  • She’s been working on her thesis for over a year.
  • It has been raining since early this morning.

Quick practice – Present Perfect Continuous

1. The IT technician (work) on the new website all afternoon.
2. How long you (learn) English?
3. My eyes hurt because I (mark) essays for the last three hours.

5. Past Perfect

The “past before the past” – an action completed before another past action or point in time.

When I arrived, the lesson had already started. · She hadn’t finished the exercise before the bell rang.

already · just · before · after · by the time · never

Note: Past Perfect is usually paired with Past Simple to make the order of two past events unambiguous.

  • By the time we arrived, the meeting had already started.
  • She had never seen snow before she moved to Slovakia.
  • He had finished his homework before dinner.
  • They had already left when I called.
  • I realised I had forgotten my passport.
  • The film had already begun when we got to the cinema.
  • She was tired because she hadn’t slept well.
  • We had booked the tickets before the price went up.
  • By 2020, the school had completed three Erasmus+ projects.
  • He had studied English for years before he moved abroad.

Quick practice – Past Perfect

1. By the time the inspector arrived, we (already / prepare) all the documents.
2. She was upset because she (not / receive) the results yet.
3. When the bell rang, the teacher (just / finish) explaining the homework.

Common time expressions at a glance

TenseTypical signal words
Present Simplealways, usually, often, every day/week, on Mondays, never
Present Continuousnow, right now, at the moment, currently, this week, these days
Past Simpleyesterday, last week/year, in 2015, ago, when + past time
Past Continuouswhile, as, when (+ interrupting action), at 8 o’clock yesterday
Present Perfectalready, just, yet, ever, never, since, for, so far, recently
Present Perfect Continuousfor, since, all day/morning, recently, lately, how long…?
Past Perfectalready, just, before, after, by the time, never, when (+ two past events)

Memory hack

Happening NOW → simple/continuous present.   FINISHED at a stated time → past simple.   UNFINISHED or result NOW → present perfect.   BEFORE another past action → past perfect.

Part 1

Science & Tech Today

Transportation

Getting from A to B is being transformed by rapid technological change. Electric vehicles (EVs) are no longer a niche product — many major manufacturers are phasing out petrol and diesel engines altogether, driven by both environmental pressure and falling battery costs. Meanwhile, autonomous vehicles are being tested on public roads in several countries, promising to reduce human error, which causes the vast majority of road accidents.

Beyond cars, delivery drones are already being used to transport parcels and even medical supplies to remote areas, while engineers continue to develop hyperloop systems — sealed tubes in which pods could theoretically travel at airline speeds, cutting a journey between two cities from hours to minutes.

High-speed rail networks are also being extended across Europe and Asia, offering a lower-carbon alternative to short-haul flights. Whether these innovations will be adopted quickly enough to meet climate targets remains to be seen, but the direction of change is clear: transport is being reinvented from the ground up.

Medicine

Medicine has always been shaped by technology, but the pace of change today is remarkable. Robotic surgery allows operations to be performed with a level of precision that would be impossible for the human hand alone, often through incisions just a few millimetres wide, which shortens recovery time considerably.

Artificial intelligence is increasingly used to analyse scans and detect conditions such as cancer earlier than a doctor might, simply because it can be trained on millions of images at once. Telemedicine, accelerated by the pandemic, means that patients in rural areas can now be examined by specialists hundreds of kilometres away, without ever leaving home.

Perhaps the most far-reaching development is gene editing. Techniques such as CRISPR allow faulty genes to be corrected at a molecular level, opening the door to treatments for previously incurable inherited diseases. Wearable devices, from smartwatches to continuous glucose monitors, mean that a patient's vital signs can now be tracked around the clock, often before symptoms are even noticed.

IT & Artificial Intelligence

Few developments have moved as fast as artificial intelligence. Tools such as ChatGPT can now be used to draft emails, summarise documents, or even write code, and they are being integrated into everyday software at a startling rate. Behind the scenes, this is only possible because of cloud computing, which allows enormous amounts of data to be stored and processed remotely rather than on a single device.

At the same time, the Internet of Things means that everyday objects — fridges, thermostats, doorbells — are being connected to the internet and to each other, generating vast quantities of data. This convenience comes with a cost: as more of our lives are conducted online, cybersecurity has become essential, since personal information can be stolen or misused if systems aren't properly protected.

Looking further ahead, quantum computing is being developed to solve problems that would take a classical computer thousands of years, from designing new medicines to modelling climate change. Whether it lives up to its promise is still being determined by researchers around the world.

Compound Nouns — Six Building Patterns

Compounds combine two words into one idea. English builds them in several predictable ways — learn the pattern, and new compounds get much easier to guess.

PatternExamples
1. Noun + Noundatabase, keyboard, data breach, gene therapy, space station
2. Gerund + Nouncharging station, parking sensor, operating system, processing power
3. Adjective + Nounartificial intelligence, renewable energy, smart device, electric vehicle
4. Noun/Adj/Adv + Past Participlebattery-powered, solar-heated, computer-generated, remote-controlled, self-driven
5. Noun/Adj/Adv + Present Participlefast-growing, far-reaching, self-driving, long-lasting, groundbreaking
6. Number/Adj + Noun + −ed/−dthree-wheeled, high-powered, wide-angled, flat-screened
One word, two words, or hyphenated? There's no perfect rule, but some patterns help: compounds that have been common for a long time tend to fuse into one word (smartphone, database, keyboard). A compound adjective placed before a noun is usually hyphenated (a well-known scientist), but often not when it comes after the noun it describes (the scientist is well known). Newer or more technical compounds are frequently left as two separate words (data breach, gene therapy, artificial intelligence). When in doubt, a dictionary is more reliable than a rule.

Part 3 · Grammar

Language Lab: The Passive Voice

Every tense, questions, formal instructions, and the exceptions that trip students up — especially useful for science and news writing, which lean heavily on the passive.

What is the passive voice?

In an active sentence, the subject performs the action. In a passive sentence, the object of the action becomes the subject, and the doer becomes optional. Compare:

Active: Engineers test the device.
Passive: The device is tested (by engineers).

Form: a form of "be" + past participle.

We use the passive when the action or the result matters more than who did it — because the doer is unknown, unimportant, obvious, or simply because the style is formal, scientific, or journalistic. Science and technology writing uses the passive constantly, as you'll have noticed in the reading texts above.

1. Passive in every tense

Tense / formStructureExample
Present Simpleis / are + PPThe results are checked every week.
Present Continuousis / are being + PPThe prototype is being tested this week.
Present Perfecthas / have been + PPThe software has been updated.
Past Simplewas / were + PPThe vaccine was developed in record time.
Past Continuouswas / were being + PPThe bridge was being repaired when it collapsed.
Past Perfecthad been + PPThe device had already been tested before release.
Future Simplewill be + PPThe new model will be launched next year.
Future Perfectwill have been + PPBy 2030, the system will have been fully automated.
Modal + passivemodal + be + PPThe results must be verified.
going tois / are going to be + PPThe satellite is going to be launched in June.

Present Perfect Continuous and Future Continuous passives exist in theory but are rarely used — English usually rephrases these instead.

2. Passive questions

Yes/No: auxiliary + subject + past participle.
Is the device tested regularly?  ·  Was the report written on time?  ·  Has the bridge been repaired?

Wh-questions: question word + auxiliary + subject + past participle.
When was the device tested?  ·  How often is the software updated?  ·  Who was the prize awarded to?

3. Passive imperatives, commands & formal instructions

True passive imperatives are rare in everyday English, but they matter for formal, technical and regulatory language — exactly the register used in manuals, laws and exam texts.

StructureExample
Let + object + be + PPLet the results be published as soon as possible.
be + to be + PP (formal regulation)The form is to be completed in black ink.
Get + PP (informal, dynamic)Get vaccinated. Get your device checked.
Note: everyday instructions usually stay active ("Switch off the device") or use "should/must be" rather than a true passive imperative — reserve "Let X be done" for formal or literary contexts.

4. Exceptions — what to watch out for

No object, no passive: intransitive verbs like arrive, happen, occur, exist, disappear, die and fall cannot be passivised — there's no object to promote to subject. "An accident happened," never "was happened."
State/possession verbs: have, own, resemble, lack, fit, suit, cost, weigh are rarely passivised with their literal meaning. "The device weighs two kilos," not "is weighed two kilos."
Two-object verbs give two passives: "The company gave the engineer an award" → "The engineer was given an award" or "An award was given to the engineer."
Impersonal reporting passives are common in science and news writing: "It is believed that AI will transform medicine" / "AI is believed to transform medicine."
get-passive vs. be-passive: "get" is more informal and dynamic, often for accidents or changes — "The laptop got damaged in transit" vs. the more formal "The laptop was damaged in transit."
Prepositional verbs keep their preposition at the end: "The problem was looked into." "The issue is being dealt with."
Causative "have/get something done" looks similar to a passive but means arranging for someone to do something for you: "We had the system upgraded" (we arranged it) is different from the true passive "The system was upgraded."

Language Lab 2: The Impersonal Passive

"It is believed that…", "The drug is thought to have been…": reporting structures are everywhere in science news, medical reports and technology articles. They let the writer report opinions and facts without saying who holds them.

Two patterns, one meaning

Active: Scientists believe that the new vaccine protects against all variants.

Pattern A (It + passive + that-clause): It is believed that the new vaccine protects against all variants.

Pattern B (Subject + passive + to-infinitive): The new vaccine is believed to protect against all variants.

Pattern B is shorter and very common in formal writing. The subject of the that-clause (the new vaccine) becomes the subject of the whole sentence.

1. Verbs used in impersonal passives

Both patterns (It… / Subject…)Only Pattern A (It… that)
say, think, believe, know, consider, expect, report, claim, estimate, understand, allege, assume, fear, suppose, find, acknowledgehope, suggest, propose, recommend, agree, decide, argue, announce
The robot is said to work 24 hours a day.It is hoped that the treatment will be cheaper.
✗ The treatment is hoped to be cheaper.

2. The key rule: choose the right infinitive

Compare two moments: the time of the reporting verb (is said, was believed) and the time of the action you are reporting.

Relation to the reporting verbInfinitiveExample
Same time / general truthto + verbThe scanner is believed to detect tumours earlier.
Same time, happening nowto be + -ingEngineers are reported to be testing a new maglev train.
Same time, passiveto be + past participleThe drug is expected to be approved next year.
Earlier actionto have + past participlePenicillin is estimated to have saved over 200 million lives.
Earlier action, lastingto have been + -ingThe hackers are thought to have been operating for months.
Earlier action, passiveto have been + past participleThe crash is thought to have been caused by a software error.

Future: with expect (and often believe, think), a simple infinitive can refer to the future: Air taxis are expected to start commercial flights soon. With other verbs, use Pattern A with will: It is believed that 6G will be introduced around 2030.

3. Step by step: from active to impersonal

StepExample
1. Active sentenceExperts think that the satellite was damaged by space debris.
2. Pattern A: It + be + past participle + thatIt is thought that the satellite was damaged by space debris.
3. Move the subject of the that-clause to the frontThe satellite is thought…
4. Earlier and passive → to have been + PPThe satellite is thought to have been damaged by space debris.

If the reporting verb is in the past, keep it in the past: People believed that the drug was safe → The drug was believed to be safe. (Both happened at the same time, so we use the simple infinitive.)

4. There + impersonal passive

It is thought that there is a link between screen time and poor sleep.
→ There is thought to be a link between screen time and poor sleep.
It is said that there were problems with the first prototype.
→ There are said to have been problems with the first prototype.

💡 Hack 1: Two clocks. Always ask: did the action happen at the same time as the reporting, or before it? Same time → to do / to be doing / to be done. Before → to have done / to have been doing / to have been done.
💡 Hack 2: Copy the verb from the that-clause. The verb in the that-clause tells you which infinitive to use: is testing → to be testing, has saved → to have saved, was caused → to have been caused, had been working → to have been working.
💡 Hack 3: Don't drop "be". The reporting verb itself must be passive: The phone is said to…, not The phone says to…. This is the most common mistake in tests.
💡 Hack 4: "Hope", "suggest", "recommend" only work with "It". It is hoped that… ✓ / The cure is hoped to… ✗
💡 Hack 5: Intransitive verbs stay active in the infinitive. The virus is believed to have originated in bats, not to have been originated: "originate", "happen" and "occur" have no passive.
💡 Hack 6: Word-count transformations. In keyword tasks (3–6 words), contractions count as two words, and the key word must not be changed. Structures like is thought to have been use up many words, so check your count.
💡 Hack 7: "Be supposed to" has two meanings. You are supposed to wear a mask = you should. The app is supposed to be very secure = people say it is.
🇸🇰 For Slovak speakers: Slovak often uses vraj, údajne or predpokladá sa, že…. For example, Údajne ho vyvinuli v Japonsku = It is said to have been developed in Japan. Don't translate word for word: ✗ It is said him to have…

Reading Comprehension

An exam-style text on robotic surgery, AI, and the ethical questions they raise — with the three question types you'll meet in the Maturita exam.

When Machines Take the Knife: Technology, Ethics and the Future

A patient lies on the operating table while, a few metres away, a surgeon watches a screen and guides a set of robotic arms with millimetre precision. This scene, once the stuff of science fiction, is now a routine part of many hospitals. Robotic surgery allows procedures to be carried out through incisions just a few millimetres wide, which means patients typically recover faster and experience less pain than after traditional operations. Critics point out, however, that the equipment is extremely expensive, and that not every hospital — particularly in poorer regions — can afford it.

Artificial intelligence is transforming medicine in other ways too. Algorithms trained on millions of medical images can now spot early signs of cancer that a tired or inexperienced doctor might miss. The same technology is reshaping classrooms: tools such as ChatGPT can already draft essays, explain difficult concepts, and even mark simple exercises. Teachers are divided over what this means. Some welcome AI as a tireless personal tutor, available to every student around the clock; others worry that reaching for an AI-generated answer too quickly could quietly undermine students' creativity, patience and independent thinking.

These questions belong to a wider ethical debate. If a self-driving car has to choose between two accidents, who decides which one is preferable — the engineer, the company, or the law? Should scientists be allowed to alter the genetic code of a human embryo, even to prevent a serious illness? Is it right to develop technology that could, in theory, extend human life indefinitely, when so many basic needs elsewhere remain unmet? None of these questions has an easy answer, and that is precisely the point: as technology accelerates, society's ability to agree on its limits often lags behind.

What seems certain is that science and technology will keep reshaping transport, medicine and daily life at a pace few could have predicted a generation ago. Whether the result is mostly beneficial will depend less on the machines themselves than on the choices — political, economic and personal — that humans make about how to use them.

Listening: A Day in the Life of a Robotics Engineer

While you listen: complete the notes

Interviewer: So, tell us — what does a normal day look like for you?

Engineer: Honestly, no two days are the same! Most mornings, I start by checking the robots that are being tested in the lab. Right now, we're working on a new surgical arm — it's still a prototype, so it has to be tested constantly. If anything goes wrong overnight, it gets flagged automatically, and that's usually the first thing I look at.

Interviewer: And what's the most exciting part of the job?

Engineer: Probably seeing an idea go from a sketch on a whiteboard to something that actually works. A few years ago, this robotic arm existed only as a drawing. Now it's being used — well, tested — in real operating theatres. That's pretty amazing, when you think about it.

Interviewer: Do you ever worry about the ethical side of things? Robots making medical decisions, that sort of thing?

Engineer: All the time, actually. We're really careful that the robot never makes the decision — it's always controlled by a surgeon. The robot just makes their hands steadier, basically. But yeah, as this technology develops, I think those conversations are only going to get more important.

Interviewer: What advice would you give to a student thinking about going into robotics?

Engineer: Get comfortable with not knowing the answer straight away. Things break, tests fail, and you learn more from that than from anything going right the first time. And don't just focus on the engineering — read about the ethics too. You'll need both.

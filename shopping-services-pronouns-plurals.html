💡 Watch out: easily confused words

  • a receipt (proof that you paid) · a recipe (instructions for cooking) · a prescription (a doctor's order for medicine).
  • the bill (in a restaurant, BrE) · the check (AmE) · an invoice (a business bill).
  • accommodation, luggage, advice, information, furniture are uncountable – no a, no plural.
  • shopping = buying things · a shop (BrE) / a store (AmE) · do the shopping · go shopping.
  • cost (it costs €10) · price (the price is €10) · prize (what you win!).
  • have your hair cut = sb else cuts it · cut your hair = you cut it yourself.

📰 Reading: Is the High Street Dying?

Read about the future of shops and services. It contains all the grammar of this lesson – you will find it in Exercise 7.

1It is often said that the high street is dying. In many towns, shops that families had run for generations have closed, and passers-by see more empty windows than displays. Online retailers, whose prices are hard to beat, have changed the way we shop. Many people simply can't imagine queuing at a checkout any more when they can order everything from their sofa.

2But the story is more complicated than that. Researchers studying shopping habits have noticed a surprising phenomenon: young people, who grew up with smartphones, are returning to physical shops. They want experiences, not just products. A café inside a bookshop, a repair workshop in a clothes store, or a market where customers and farmers know each other – these are the things that bring people back.

3Services are changing, too. Banks have replaced cashiers with apps, post offices have become parcel lockers, and hotels let guests check themselves in on a tablet. Some customers enjoy doing everything by themselves; others, especially older people, feel left behind. "I don't want to talk to a machine," one pensioner told a reporter. "I want to talk to someone who knows me."

4The criteria by which we judge a good shop are therefore shifting. Price still matters, of course, but so do friendly staff, ethical products and a sense of community. Independent shops cannot compete with the giants on price, but they can offer something the big ones can't: a personal touch.

5All in all, the high street is not dead – it is changing. Shops that are just places to pay will probably disappear. Those that become places to meet, learn and enjoy themselves are likely to survive. In a nutshell, the future of shopping may look surprisingly like its past.

🔁 Pronouns: Reciprocal, Relative, Reflexive and Substitute

Four kinds of pronouns that help you avoid repetition – and four kinds of typical mistakes.

🔁 Reciprocal: each other / one another

  • A does sth to B and B to A
  • The two shop owners help each other.
  • one another – more formal, often for 3+ people
  • Possessive: We borrow each other's clothes.

🪞 Reflexive: myself, yourself…

  • subject = object: She cut herself.
  • emphasis: I made it myself. (nobody helped)
  • by myself = alone: He lives by himself.
  • Help yourself! · Enjoy yourselves! · Behave yourself!

🔗 Relative: who, which, that, whose…

  • The assistant who served me…
  • The shop, whose owner is French, …
  • …, which was a shock.
  • See the Housing module for the full lab.

🔄 Substitute: one / ones, so / not

  • one / ones replace a countable noun: I don't like the red bag. I'll take the black one.
  • so / not replace a clause: Is it open? – I think so. / I hope not.
  • do so (formal): Customers may return goods if they wish to do so.

🚫 Verbs that are NOT reflexive in English

relax · feel · concentrate · meet · hurry · wonder · complain · decide · remember · get dressed · get up · sit down · wash / shave (usually)
Relax! (not Relax yourself) · I feel tired. (not I feel myself tired) · We met at the mall. · Hurry up! · I can't concentrate.

⚖️ themselves vs each other

They looked at themselves in the mirror. → each person looked at himself / herself.
They looked at each other. → A looked at B and B looked at A.

⚠️ Typical mistakes

  • I'm going to relax myself in the spa. → I'm going to relax in the spa.
  • They love themselves. (when you mean the couple) → They love each other.
  • I'll take the blue. → I'll take the blue one. (an adjective alone can't replace a noun)
  • I think yes. / I don't think. → I think so. / I don't think so.
  • theirselves, hisself → themselves, himself

🔤 Irregular Plurals: Compound Nouns and Foreign Words

Where does the -s go in mother-in-law? What is the plural of crisis? Learn the patterns once and for all.

Compound nouns: the MAIN word takes -s

  • a passer-by → passers-by
  • a mother-in-law → mothers-in-law
  • a runner-up → runners-up
  • an editor-in-chief → editors-in-chief
  • a looker-on → lookers-on

Compound nouns: the LAST word takes -s

  • a shop assistant → shop assistants
  • a toothbrush → toothbrushes
  • a grown-up → grown-ups
  • a take-off → take-offs
  • a check-in → check-ins

man / woman as the first word

  • BOTH change:
  • a woman doctor → women doctors
  • a man servant → men servants
  • but: a salesman → salesmen

Latin and Greek words

  • -us → -i: cactus → cacti, stimulus → stimuli, fungus → fungi
  • -is → -es: crisis → crises, analysis → analyses, thesis → theses
  • -on → -a: criterion → criteria, phenomenon → phenomena
  • -um → -a: medium → media, datum → data, bacterium → bacteria
  • -a → -ae: formula → formulae (also formulas), larva → larvae
  • -ix / -ex → -ices: appendix → appendices, index → indices

Old irregular plurals

  • man → men, woman → women, child → children
  • foot → feet, tooth → teeth, goose → geese
  • mouse → mice, ox → oxen, person → people
  • same form: sheep, deer, fish, species, series, aircraft

French words

  • bureau → bureaux (or bureaus)
  • gâteau → gâteaux
  • chateau → chateaux

⚠️ Watch out

  • data and media are often used with a singular verb in everyday English: The data is / are clear. Both are accepted.
  • news, information, advice look plural or are uncountable – always singular: The news is good.
  • police, people, cattle are always plural: The police are coming.
  • a criterion / a phenomenon (singular) – a criteria ❌

🗞️ Impersonal Passive Constructions (Revision)

Typical of news reports and formal writing: It is said that… and …is said to….

Pattern 1: It + passive + that

It is said that the mall is the biggest in Europe.

It was reported that the shop had closed.

Verbs: say, think, believe, report, expect, know, consider, claim, estimate

Pattern 2: Subject + passive + to-infinitive

The mall is said to be the biggest in Europe. (same time)

The shop is reported to have closed. (earlier)

Prices are expected to be rising. (in progress)

🔎 Revision

  • Same time → to + infinitive · earlier → to have + past participle · in progress → to be + -ing.
  • For a full lab, see the Science and Technology – Impersonal Passive module.

🧾 Summarising Phrases

Every essay, review and complaint needs a good ending. Choose the phrase that matches your style.

PhraseStyleExample
To sum up, … / In conclusion, …formal, essaysTo sum up, online shopping has both advantages and disadvantages.
All in all, … / On the whole, …neutralAll in all, it was a great weekend at the spa.
By and large, …neutral = mostly, generallyBy and large, customers are satisfied with the service.
In short, … / In brief, …neutralIn short, the hotel was a disaster.
Taking everything into account, … / All things considered, …formalAll things considered, local shops deserve our support.
In a nutshell, …informalIn a nutshell, it's cheap but not cheerful.
To cut a long story short, …informal, storiesTo cut a long story short, we never got our money back.
The bottom line is (that) …informal, the most important pointThe bottom line is that we can't afford it.
At the end of the day, …spokenAt the end of the day, the customer is always right.

⚠️ Watch out

  • In the end = finally, after a long time (In the end, we bought the cheaper one.) – it doesn't summarise an essay. Use In conclusion.
  • At last = after waiting (with relief), not for summarising.
  • Avoid informal phrases (in a nutshell, to cut a long story short) in a formal essay or letter of complaint.

Discussion Lab

Three consumer dilemmas

Work in pairs or small groups. Choose one dilemma, add your own arguments and reach a conclusion together.

🛒 Online or in a real shop?

+ Online: cheaper, 24/7, huge choice

− Online: can't try things on, delivery problems

+ Shop: personal advice, take it home now

− Shop: queues, limited opening hours

🏪 Supermarket chain or local shop?

+ Chain: low prices, loyalty cards

− Chain: impersonal, kills small businesses

+ Local: friendly, supports the community

− Local: more expensive, smaller choice

🤖 Machines or people?

+ Machines: fast, no waiting, cheap

− Machines: older people feel excluded

+ People: help, smiles, advice

− People: higher prices, limited hours

🗨️ Useful phrases

  • Complaining politely: I'm afraid there's a problem with… · I'd like to speak to the manager, please. · Could you possibly…?
  • Asking for service: I'd like to make an appointment for… · Could we have the bill, please? · Do you have this in a bigger size?
  • Summarising: All in all, … · By and large, … · The bottom line is…
Speaking Exam · Questions

Answer in 1–2 minutes each

Shopping

  • Where do you usually shop and why?
  • Are you a shopaholic or do you hate shopping?
  • Have you ever bought something on impulse and regretted it?

Paying

  • Do you prefer paying in cash or by card?
  • Will cash disappear in the future?
  • Have you ever returned something to a shop?

Services

  • Describe the best or worst service you have received.
  • How has the post office changed?
  • Would you like to work in a hotel or restaurant?

Beauty & health

  • Why are spas and wellness centres so popular?
  • Do people spend too much on their appearance?
  • What do you think about cosmetic procedures for teenagers?

🗣️ Warm-up: Let's Talk

Before you look at any grammar, discuss these questions with a partner or think them through out loud. This is exactly the kind of speaking task you'll meet in the maturita exam.

👤 Youth

  • What springs to mind when you hear the word "young"?
  • What three adjectives would you use to describe today's youth?
  • Is being young today easier or harder than in your parents' generation?

⚠️ Issues affecting young people

  • Which problems do you think are most serious for teenagers today?
  • What shapes the differences between generations — social, economic, political or technological change?
  • What could be done to lessen the negative effects of these issues?

🌟 Role models and idols

  • Why is it important — or not — to have a role model?
  • Name someone who could serve as a positive example for young people. Justify your choice.
  • Can a role model also influence someone negatively? How?

🔮 Young people and their future

  • Where do you see yourself in ten years?
  • What are your hopes and fears about the future?
  • How do you plan to achieve your aims?
"Your words may fall on deaf ears, but you are always being observed. Be a role model." Discuss: do you agree? Give reasons.

🧩 Grammar Focus: Conditionals

Conditional sentences describe the result of a condition. There are five main types you need for B2 level — including the two Mixed Conditionals.

Zero Conditional — general truths & facts

If + present simple, present simple
Used for things that are always true (facts, rules, habits). "If young people spend too much time online, they get less sleep."

First Conditional — real, possible future

If + present simple, will + infinitive
A realistic condition and its likely future result. "If teenagers don't find a role model, they will struggle to form their identity." You can also use can, may or an imperative instead of will.

Second Conditional — unreal present/future

If + past simple, would + infinitive
An imaginary, unlikely or impossible situation now or in the future. "If teenagers had more free time, they would pursue their real passions." In formal English, use were for all persons: "If I were you, I would talk to someone I trust."

Third Conditional — unreal past (no result now)

If + past perfect, would have + past participle
A hypothetical change to the past that can no longer happen. "If she had had a better role model, she would have made different choices."

Mixed Conditional Type 1 — past condition → present result

If + past perfect, would + infinitive (no "have")
An unreal past action affects the present. "If he hadn't dropped out of school, he would have a better job now."

Mixed Conditional Type 2 — general/present condition → past result

If + past simple, would have + past participle
A permanent characteristic or general truth affects a past event. "If she weren't so shy, she would have spoken up at the debate."

⚡ Exceptions & alternatives to remember

  • Unless = if...not: "Unless young people find purpose, they feel lost."
  • Provided (that) / as long as / on condition that can replace if in the First Conditional, often in more formal contexts: "You can go out, provided that you finish your homework."
  • Inversion (formal, no if): Were I you, I would talk to a counsellor. / Had I known, I would have acted differently. / Should you need help, contact your teacher.
  • In the Second and Third Conditionals, could or might can replace would to show ability or possibility rather than certainty.

💭 Grammar Focus: Wish Clauses & If Only

We use wish (and the more emphatic if only) to talk about things we would like to be different — in the present, in the past, or about someone's behaviour.

Wish + past simple — regret about the present

wish + past simple
A present situation we would like to change. "I wish I had more free time." Use were instead of was in all persons in a more formal/careful style: "I wish I were older."

Wish + past perfect — regret about the past

wish + past perfect
Something in the past that we would like to have been different — it cannot be changed now. "I wish I hadn't wasted my youth glued to a screen."

Wish + would + infinitive — irritation / a wish for change in others

wish + would + infinitive
Used to complain about someone else's behaviour, or to wish something would happen/change. "I wish my parents would stop comparing me to others."

Wish + could — desire for ability or permission

wish + could + infinitive
Wanting to be able to do something, now or in general. "I wish I could travel more before starting university."

If only

if only + past simple / past perfect / would
A stronger, more emotional version of wish, following exactly the same rules. "If only teenagers felt less pressure to be perfect online!"
StructureTime referenceMeaning
wish + past simplepresentregret about now
wish + past perfectpastregret about the past
wish + would + infinitivefuture / habitualirritation, wish for change (not for yourself)
wish + couldpresent/futurelack of ability or permission

⚡ Exceptions & common mistakes

  • Wish + would is not normally used about yourself — you can't tell yourself to change; use wish + past simple instead: ✗ I wish I would be taller. → ✓ I wish I were taller.
  • Wish + would is not usually used with stative verbs (know, like, be, understand) — use the past simple instead.
  • After wish, never use will — only would.
  • Hope is different from wish: hope is used for things that are still possible (+ present/future tense), while wish is used for things that are unreal, unlikely or impossible.
